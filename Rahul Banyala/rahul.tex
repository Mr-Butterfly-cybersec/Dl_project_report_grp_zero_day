\begin{abstract}

This study deals with the real-time recognition of people using CNN-based deep learning models. It reviews and compares four different architectures: LightCNN-9, FaceNet, VGGFace, and MobileNetV1. The comparison uses the experimental results and figures reported in published research papers. Wherever possible, results obtained using the same facial dataset and evaluation protocol are selected to maintain a fair comparison. The models are compared based on recognition accuracy, computational requirements, inference speed, and suitability for real-time use. The study aims to identify which architecture provides the best balance between recognition performance and computational efficiency for a real-time facial recognition system. Later, the best-performing model will be used to build a real-time face recognition system using our own dataset.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Facial recognition is a domain of computer vision that deals with recognising a human face and assigning a label or identity to it. It is commonly used in security systems, access control, attendance monitoring, surveillance, mobile-device authentication, and the organisation of digital images. Unlike face detection, which only finds the location of a face within an image, facial recognition attempts to determine the identity of the detected person. In a real-time facial recognition system, this complete process must be performed quickly enough to recognise people from a live image or video stream. Therefore, such a system requires not only good recognition accuracy but also efficient processing and a suitable response time.

The major challenge in facial recognition lies in the large variety of human faces and the different conditions in which facial images can be captured. Changes in the surrounding environment, lighting, facial position, camera angle, facial expression, and distance from the camera can significantly affect recognition performance. Other factors, such as low image resolution, image size, partial facial obstruction, background variation, ageing, and poor camera quality, can make the task even more difficult. The same person may appear different under different conditions, while two different people may sometimes have similar facial features. A reliable facial recognition model must therefore learn the important features that represent a person's identity while reducing the effect of these unwanted variations.

Within this field, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have become one of the major deep learning models used for recognising and classifying images. CNNs learn features directly from image data through hierarchical representations. The earlier layers generally learn simple features such as edges, lines, and textures, while the deeper layers learn more complex patterns such as facial structures and identity-related features. Their local connectivity and shared-weight mechanism allow them to process images efficiently and make them highly versatile for different image-related tasks. However, different CNN architectures may produce different results in terms of recognition accuracy, model size, computational requirements, and processing speed.

In this study, four CNN-based architectures are reviewed and compared for facial recognition: LightCNN-9, FaceNet, VGGFace, and MobileNetV1. LightCNN-9 is a compact facial recognition architecture that uses the Max-Feature-Map activation to select more informative features and reduce the effect of less useful information. Its relatively lightweight structure makes it an important candidate for a real-time facial recognition system where computational efficiency is required. The remaining architectures are also studied to understand how their designs influence recognition performance, efficiency, and suitability for real-time use.

The comparison is based on the experimental results and figures reported in the original research papers. To make the comparison as fair as possible, results obtained using similar datasets, evaluation protocols, preprocessing methods, and performance metrics are selected wherever they are available. Results from different testing conditions are not treated as directly equivalent, and these differences are considered while discussing the performance of each architecture. The models are compared using factors such as recognition accuracy, inference speed, model size, and computational requirements.

The main purpose of this study is to identify the architecture that provides the best balance between recognition performance and computational efficiency. The best-performing and most suitable model will later be selected to build a real-time facial recognition system trained on our own dataset. This comparison will therefore provide the foundation for choosing a model that can recognise the required individuals accurately while still being practical for real-time implementation.

\section{Architecture 1: LightCNN-9}

LightCNN was introduced by Wu et al. to learn facial features from large-scale datasets containing incorrectly labelled and noisy images \cite{wu2018lightcnn}. The main objective of this architecture is to achieve reliable facial recognition while reducing the model size and computational requirements. LightCNN-9 is one of the smaller architectures proposed in the original study, making it suitable for systems where recognition speed and available hardware are important.

The main feature of LightCNN is the Max-Feature-Map (MFM) operation, which is used instead of a conventional activation function such as ReLU. A convolutional layer first produces $2N$ feature maps, which are divided into pairs. MFM compares the corresponding values in each pair and keeps the maximum value:

\begin{equation}
\hat{x}_{ij}^{k}=\max\left(x_{ij}^{k},x_{ij}^{k+N}\right)
\end{equation}

As a result, $2N$ input feature maps are reduced to $N$ output feature maps. This creates competition between the feature maps and allows the network to retain the stronger facial information while suppressing weaker or noisy responses.

LightCNN-9 takes a $128 \times 128$ grayscale facial image as its input. The first stage contains a $5 \times 5$ convolution followed by MFM and max pooling. It is followed by four convolution blocks, with each block containing a $1 \times 1$ convolution, MFM, a $3 \times 3$ convolution, and another MFM operation. Four max-pooling operations gradually reduce the spatial dimensions to $8 \times 8 \times 128$. The resulting features are flattened into 8,192 values and passed through a fully connected layer containing 512 outputs. MFM then reduces this output to a compact 256-dimensional facial representation. Finally, a fully connected classifier produces a score for each identity class. The complete flow of the architecture is presented in Figure~\ref{fig:lightcnn9_architecture}.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/lightcnn9_publication.pdf}
    \caption{Architecture of LightCNN-9 showing the convolution blocks, MFM operations, pooling layers, 256-dimensional embedding, and identity classifier.}
    \label{fig:lightcnn9_architecture}
\end{figure*}

According to the original paper, LightCNN-9 contains approximately 5.556 million parameters and requires approximately 1 GFLOP. It produces a 256-dimensional facial representation and requires approximately 67 ms for feature extraction on a single core of an Intel i7-4790 processor. The paper reports 98.80\% accuracy on LFW and 93.40\% accuracy on YTF under its stated evaluation protocols \cite{wu2018lightcnn}.

The smaller model size, low-dimensional representation, and use of grayscale images make LightCNN-9 a suitable candidate for real-time facial recognition. However, its fully connected layer requires a fixed input size, and the reported processing time depends on the hardware used by the original authors. The LFW result also represents face verification rather than the identity-classification task required by our final system. For our implementation, the final classifier can be replaced with a new classifier for the identities in our own dataset while retaining the earlier layers for facial feature extraction.

\section{Architecture 2: FaceNet}

FaceNet is a unified system for face verification, recognition, and clustering, which uses a deep convolutional network trained with triplet loss. Each face is mapped onto the Euclidean space. The network is trained such that the embeddings of similar data points converge closer together within the feature space, while simultaneously pushing embeddings of dissimilar data points to drift further apart; meaning images of the same person are close together in the learned space and vice versa in the case of images of different people \cite{schroff2015facenet}.

It also uses a triplet loss function. To calculate triplet loss, we use images corresponding to the anchor, positive, and negative.

\begin{center}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth,trim=34 15 25 19,clip]{figures/facenet_model_structure.png}
    \captionof{figure}{Model Structure of FaceNet}
    \label{fig:facenet_model_structure}
\end{center}

The network consists of an input layer, a deep CNN, followed by L2 normalization, which results in the face embedding. This is then followed by the triplet loss.

\noindent\underline{Triplet Loss and Selection}

The loss function is defined using triplets of training points of the form (A, P, N).

A = anchor (reference image)\\
P = positive (if the image is of the same person as A)\\
N = negative (if the image is of a different person than A)

Triplet loss minimizes the distance between the anchor and the positive, while maximizing the distance between the anchor and the negative (see Figure~\ref{fig:facenet_triplet_loss}).

\begin{center}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/facenet_triplet_loss.png}
    \captionof{figure}{Triplet Loss}
    \label{fig:facenet_triplet_loss}
\end{center}

Triplet loss can be formalized as:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
L=\sum_i^N\big[&\left\|f(x_i^a)-f(x_i^p)\right\|_2^2 \\
&-\left\|f(x_i^a)-f(x_i^n)\right\|_2^2+\alpha\big]_+
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:facenet_triplet_loss}
\end{equation}

$\alpha$ here is defined as the margin that is enforced between positive and negative pairs.

Generating all possible triplets would result in multiple triplets that already satisfy the constraint. These triplets would not contribute to the training and would result in slower convergence. And thus it is important to select hard triplets which can therefore contribute to improving the model.

\noindent\underline{Deep Convolution Networks}\par\nopagebreak[4]

FaceNet trains CNNs using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with standard backpropagation and AdaGrad.

SGD is an optimization algorithm used to reduce the loss function.

\begin{center}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/facenet_sdg.png}
    \captionof{figure}{SGD (Credit - \textit{datascience.stackexchange})}
    \label{fig:facenet_sdg}
\end{center}

The x-axis and y-axis represent the weights, while the z-axis is the corresponding loss. SGD updates the weights to move towards lower loss, although it does not guarantee reaching a global minimum.

In SGD, the gradient is a vector which indicates the direction of the steepest local increase. SGD then updates the parameter in the opposite direction to minimize the loss, using gradients estimated from randomly sampled training data.

AdaGrad adapts the learning rate for each parameter using its accumulated squared gradients.

\noindent\underline{ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit)}

ReLU is a non-linear activation function. Without non-linear activation functions, even multiple layers of convolution would collapse into a single linear transformation. Also, without activation functions, complex and intricate patterns cannot be learned effectively, as the decision boundary would remain linear.

ReLU is a widely used alternative to traditional activation functions such as sigmoid and tanh. This activation function helps mitigate the vanishing gradient problem, as its gradient remains constant for positive inputs.

\begin{center}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.88\columnwidth]{figures/facenet_relu.png}
    \captionof{figure}{ReLU activation function}
    \label{fig:facenet_relu}
\end{center}

\noindent\underline{CNN Architectures}

The two types of CNN architectures used in FaceNet are Zeiler \& Fergus architecture and Inception Model.

Zeiler \& Fergus architecture - This architecture introduced a novel visualization technique that gives insight into the function of intermediate layers and the operation of classifiers in a CNN.

\begin{center}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\columnwidth]{figures/facenet_zeiler_fergus.png}
    \captionof{figure}{Zeiler \& Fergus architecture used in FaceNet}
    \label{fig:facenet_zeiler_fergus}
\end{center}

Inception Model - The architecture was first introduced by the researchers at Google to curb the exorbitant resource requirement of deep neural networks. The Inception architecture combines $1\times1$, $3\times3$ and $5\times5$ convolution filters in parallel and extracts features from the input image at different scales. The output of each filter is concatenated and passed to the next layer. This process is then repeated. This approach helps capture the local and global features while also minimizing the number of parameters required.

\begin{center}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/facenet_inception.png}
    \captionof{figure}{Inception Model used in FaceNet}
    \label{fig:facenet_inception}
\end{center}

\noindent\underline{Performance of FaceNet Model on LFW}

The model is evaluated on two modes:

Fixed center crop of the LFW provided thumbnail.

A face detector is run on the LFW thumbnails. If it fails to align the images, the LFW alignment is used.

FaceNet achieves a face-verification accuracy of \textbf{98.87\% $\pm$ 0.15} with the fixed center crop and \textbf{99.63\% $\pm$ 0.09} with extra face alignment; the uncertainties are standard errors of the mean \cite{schroff2015facenet}.

\begin{center}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.88\columnwidth]{figures/facenet_lfw_errors.png}
    \captionof{figure}{LFW errors. This shows all pairs of images that were incorrectly classified on LFW}
    \label{fig:facenet_lfw_errors}
\end{center}

\noindent\underline{Conclusion}

With a verification accuracy of \textbf{99.63\% $\pm$ 0.09} on LFW, FaceNet demonstrates the effectiveness of embedding-based approaches. This result is reported for the NN1 model, which has approximately 140 million parameters; the 7.5-million-parameter NN2 is a different Inception configuration \cite{schroff2015facenet}.

\section{Architecture 3: VGGFace}

\noindent\underline{Overview and design objective}\par\nopagebreak[4]

VGGFace is a deep convolutional network for face recognition, which was introduced in the paper ``Deep Face Recognition''. Earlier, the best face recognition systems belonged to giant companies like Google, which trained on about 200 million face images, that nobody else could access. The Oxford team wanted to show that you don't need a giant private dataset for efficient facial recognition. So, the first thing they did was to build a huge public face dataset cheaply by using web searches and automatic filtering with only a little human checking. The result was 2.6 million images of 2,622 people. Second, they trained a simple but very deep CNN on it.

\noindent\underline{Main architectural feature}\par\nopagebreak[4]

VGGFace has no new special layer. It differs from an ordinary CNN as it combines a very deep but plain architecture with a two-stage training. It stacks many small $3\times3$ convolutions with ReLU and max-pooling, and it doesn't add the extra LRN step that AlexNet had. That is why it is described as ``simple''. In stage 1, the network is trained as a 2,622-class classifier, which makes training easier and faster. In stage 2, only a final projection layer is trained with triplet loss, which pulls faces of the same person closer and pushes different people apart.

\begin{center}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/vggface_triplet_projection.png}
\end{center}

\noindent\underline{Architecture flow}\par\nopagebreak[4]

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.94\columnwidth]{figures/vggface_architecture_vertical.png}
    \caption{VGGFace architecture (adapted from Parkhi et al. \cite{parkhi2015deep}).}
    \label{fig:vggface_architecture}
    \vspace{3pt}
    \begin{minipage}{0.98\columnwidth}
        \footnotesize
        \raggedright
        \setlength{\leftmargini}{1.35em}
        \setlength{\labelsep}{0.45em}
        \begin{itemize}
            \setlength{\itemsep}{3pt}
            \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
            \setlength{\parsep}{1pt}
            \setlength{\topsep}{5pt}
            \item \textbf{Input:} $224\times224$ face image (after subtracting average training face)
            \item \textbf{Convolution blocks:} $3\times3$ conv + ReLU, with max-pooling (filters: 64 $\rightarrow$ 128 $\rightarrow$ 256 $\rightarrow$ 512 $\rightarrow$ 512)
            \item \textbf{Fully connected layers:} FC6 (4,096), FC7 (4,096), FC8 (2,622) $\rightarrow$ Softmax
            \item \textbf{Embedding:} 1,024-d vector learned from the 4,096-d descriptor (from FC7)
        \end{itemize}
    \end{minipage}
\end{figure}

As the figure shows, a $224\times224$ face image, with the average training face subtracted, passes through stacks of $3\times3$ convolutions with ReLU, separated by max-pooling layers, while the number of filters grows from 64 to 512. Three fully connected layers follow: FC6 and FC7 with 4,096 outputs, then FC8 with 2,622 outputs for the identities and a softmax. A 1,024-d embedding is later learned from the 4,096-d descriptor.

\noindent\underline{Training and evaluation setup}\par\nopagebreak[4]

The network was first trained as an identity classifier with softmax loss on the authors' dataset of 2.6 million images of 2,622 people, and a final projection was then learned with a triplet loss for the embedding. Training images were rescaled to 256 pixels on the shorter side, randomly cropped to $224\times224$ and flipped with 50\% probability. Testing used LFW and YouTube Faces, whose identities were removed from the dataset, under the unrestricted protocol. Only face verification was evaluated, classification accuracy was not reported.

\noindent\underline{Reported performance and efficiency}\par\nopagebreak[4]

Under the unrestricted protocol, the authors report 98.95\% accuracy on LFW, and 99.13\% in 100\%-EER terms with embedding learning \cite{parkhi2015deep}. On YouTube Faces, accuracy rises from 91.6\% to 97.3\% with embedding learning. A single network trained on 2.6 million images matched the state of the art closely on LFW, using far less data than FaceNet's 200 million. Training used four Titan Black GPUs (6 GB each); parameter count, FLOPs and inference time were not reported.

\noindent\underline{Strengths, limitations and suitability}\par\nopagebreak[4]

Its main strength is simplicity: a plain deep CNN reaches near state-of-the-art verification results using 2.6M training images, far fewer than FaceNet's 200M \cite{parkhi2015deep}. However, the authors needed four GPUs because of the large memory footprint, and test features were averaged over 10 crops at each of three scales. As speed and model size are not reported, we expect it to be heavy for real-time webcam use; this needs testing.

\section{Architecture 4: MobileNetV1}

\noindent\underline{Introduction}\par\nopagebreak[4]

MobileNet is a lightweight architecture developed for mobile and embedded devices, where memory and processing power are limited. It aims to keep computation low while maintaining useful accuracy \cite{howard2017mobilenets}. This makes it a suitable candidate for our facial recognition system, where we need to consider both recognition accuracy and the time taken to process a face.

\noindent\underline{Depthwise separable convolution}\par\nopagebreak[4]

In a standard convolution, filtering features and combining information from different channels happen together. MobileNet splits this into two operations. Depthwise convolution applies one filter to each input channel. Pointwise convolution then uses $1\times1$ filters to combine these outputs and produce new features \cite{howard2017mobilenets}. Figure~\ref{fig:mobilenet_convolution} shows the difference between the two blocks.

\begin{center}
    \begin{minipage}{\columnwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.94\linewidth]{figures/mobilenet_convolution.pdf}
        \captionof{figure}{Standard and depthwise separable convolution. BN denotes batch normalization. Reproduced from Howard et al. \cite{howard2017mobilenets}.}
        \label{fig:mobilenet_convolution}
    \end{minipage}
\end{center}

For example, consider an input with three channels and a layer that needs 64 output channels. A standard $3\times3$ convolution requires 1,728 weights. Splitting it into depthwise and pointwise operations reduces this to 219 weights, excluding biases and batch normalization. The output can have the same dimensions while requiring much less computation.

\noindent\underline{Computational cost and parameters}\par\nopagebreak[4]

Let $D_K$ be the kernel size, $D_F$ the feature-map size, and $M$ and $N$ the input and output channel counts. Assuming equal input and output spatial dimensions, the computation costs are \cite{howard2017mobilenets}:

\begin{equation}
C_{\text{standard}}=D_K^2MND_F^2
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{separable}}=D_K^2MD_F^2+MND_F^2
\end{equation}

For $3\times3$ kernels, the paper reports roughly eight to nine times less computation. The baseline ImageNet model contains 4.2 million parameters and requires 569 million multiply-adds, achieving 70.6\% accuracy \cite{howard2017mobilenets}. These figures describe ImageNet classification, not face verification.

\noindent\underline{Core architecture and GEMM}\par\nopagebreak[4]

MobileNet begins with a standard convolution, followed by depthwise and pointwise convolution blocks. Each convolution is followed by batch normalization and ReLU. Average pooling reduces the final feature map, and a fully connected layer followed by softmax produces class probabilities. Counting depthwise and pointwise convolutions separately, the network has 28 layers \cite{howard2017mobilenets}.

Most computation occurs in the $1\times1$ pointwise convolutions. These can directly use general matrix multiplication, or GEMM, without the memory rearrangement often needed for standard convolutions. MobileNet can therefore carry out most of its work using optimized matrix-multiplication routines \cite{howard2017mobilenets}.

\noindent\underline{Width and resolution multipliers}\par\nopagebreak[4]

The width multiplier reduces the number of channels throughout the network. For example, a multiplier of 0.5 approximately halves the channels, reducing both parameters and computation. Making the network smaller can also reduce accuracy.

The resolution multiplier reduces the input image size and the spatial size of its feature maps. This lowers computation while keeping the parameter count unchanged at a fixed width. Together, these settings allow us to adjust the model to the available resources \cite{howard2017mobilenets}. Figures~\ref{fig:mobilenet_computation} and~\ref{fig:mobilenet_parameters} show these trade-offs on ImageNet.

\begin{center}
    \begin{minipage}{\columnwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.94\linewidth]{figures/mobilenet_accuracy_computation.pdf}
        \captionof{figure}{ImageNet accuracy and computation for different MobileNet widths and input resolutions. Reproduced from Howard et al. \cite{howard2017mobilenets}.}
        \label{fig:mobilenet_computation}
    \end{minipage}
\end{center}

\begin{center}
    \begin{minipage}{\columnwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.94\linewidth]{figures/mobilenet_accuracy_parameters.pdf}

        \label{fig:mobilenet_parameters}
    \end{minipage}
\end{center}


\noindent\underline{Face verification performance}\par\nopagebreak[4]

The original MobileNet paper studies face embeddings but does not report LFW accuracy. For this comparison, we use the MobileNetV1 baseline evaluated by Chen et al. in the MobileFaceNets paper \cite{chen2018mobilefacenets}.

Their baseline was trained on CASIA-WebFace using ArcFace loss and achieves 98.63\% accuracy on LFW, with 3.2 million parameters. Inference takes 60 ms on a Snapdragon 820 CPU using four threads and NCNN \cite{chen2018mobilefacenets}. These results belong to the adapted MobileNetV1 baseline, not MobileFaceNet. YTF accuracy and the baseline's embedding dimension are not explicitly reported.

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\columnwidth}
\noindent\underline{Conclusion}\par\nopagebreak[4]

MobileNetV1 reduces computation through depthwise separable convolutions, while its width and resolution can be adjusted when resources are limited. Its reported accuracy and speed still depend on the training setup and hardware. We therefore need to test it on our own dataset and measure the complete processing time before selecting it for real-time facial recognition.
\end{minipage}

\section{Analysis and Discussion}

The four architectures are compared using published research results. LFW provides the primary face-verification benchmark, while YTF evaluates face pairs from videos with changes in pose, expression, lighting and image quality. MobileNetV1's face-verification results come from Chen et al. \cite{chen2018mobilefacenets}, rather than its original ImageNet experiment. The final comparison in Table~\ref{tab:architecture_comparison} records accuracy, parameter count, embedding dimension and inference time, with missing values and testing conditions explained alongside it.

These results help identify candidates, but differences in training, evaluation and hardware prevent a direct overall ranking. Our final selection requires tests on the same dataset and hardware, including the time needed for face detection, preprocessing and recognition.

\section{Conclusion}

This study compares LightCNN-9, FaceNet, VGGFace and MobileNetV1 for real-time facial recognition. Published results help identify candidates, but different training data, evaluation protocols and hardware prevent a direct ranking. Face verification also differs from identifying people in our webcam system. Reporting gaps limit the comparison: NN1 inference time, VGGFace's parameter count and inference time, and MobileNetV1's YTF accuracy and embedding dimension are not explicitly given in the selected sources. NR means not reported, not zero; missing values are not estimated or taken from different model variants. Since the papers do not explain every omission, we cannot assign a reason to each gap. Our final choice requires testing accuracy, model size and complete processing time on our own dataset and hardware.
